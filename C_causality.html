Social and Spatial Network Analysis with Causal Interpretation

The Social and Spatial Network Analysis with Causal Interpretation (SSNAC) framework can be used to guide the collection, management, analysis, and causal interpretation of data. It offers guidance on conceptualizing and organizing spatial and social data as networks (see Appendix A) and provides tools for descriptive (see Appendix B) and causal analysis.

We define SSNAC as a set of straightforward extensions of the currently dominant framework for conducting causal inference with observational data in epidemiology. We begin by giving an overview of this approach, which we will call classic causal inference. We extend this system to situations where data are characterized by interdependence of observational units rather than independence in the social networks section. Finally, we accommodate social and spatial data simultaneously in the social–spatial networks section. In each case, we describe the assumptions and implications of each system, illustrating with worked examples.

“We re-frame the task of statistical inference from that of learning from a growing collection of identically distributed random vectors to that of learning from a growing random vector whose entries are related through a causal structure. These assumptions allow our estimates to benefit from having more observational units, even if they collectively contribute to one realization of the statistical model.”

Classic causal inference

Classic causal inference, as elaborated by Robins (1986), Hernán and Robins (2021), and others, sharpens epidemiologic reasoning by providing a template for conducting thought experiments about population health. Typically, the researcher reasons about the likely effect of some hypothetical intervention on an outcome in a defined population. They define a set of causal estimands—quantities that describe how the outcome would differ under the intervention—and, through transparent statistical assumptions, express those estimands in terms of quantities we can calculate using observed data.

Hernán, Miguel, and James M. Robins. 2021. Causal inference. Chapman & Hall/CRC monographs on statistics & applied probability. Boca Raton: Chapman & Hall/CRC.

For example, consider a hypothetical intervention where every person in the United States receives free, unlimited access to healthcare. Under classic causal inference, we can ask whether the prevalence of diabetes would change if we implemented such an intervention. We can even ask about the extent of change and estimate that using data that have already been observed. The classic causal inference framework allows this by:

  • making causal assumptions about the relationship between healthcare access, diabetes prevalence, and any factors that determine both
  • mathematically defining the hypothetical quantities of interest and expressing them in terms of estimable quantities
  • estimating those quantities using data we have collected (or can collect)

Independence

A strong assumption made often by practitioners of classic causal inference is that of independence between observational units or groups of observational units. This assumption simplifies the mathematics involved in estimating quantities from data, but it also has profound implications for how we think about the causal process under study.

In our opening example, the independence assumption holds that one person’s access to healthcare does not affect any other person’s diabetes status. It would imply that the diabetes status for a married man who does not know how to cook would not be affected by improvements in health literacy experienced by his wife who shops and cooks for the household. This assumption runs counter to our intuitions about social life. Humans are deeply networked—our lives are defined by our relationships with one another.

Homogeneity

Accompanying the independence assumption are implicit or explicit assumptions about the homogeneity of the causal process under study. Consider two people, Tumi and Thabo, who each toss the same coin once at time 1 and again at time 2. Depending on our homogeneity assumptions, we could say that we are conducting:

  • one experiment called “coin toss” and repeating it four times
  • two experiments called “coin toss by Thabo” and “coin toss by Tumi” and repeating each experiment twice
  • two experiments called “coin toss at time 1” and “coin toss at time 2” and repeating each experiment twice
  • four experiments called “coin toss by Thabo at time 1”, “coin toss by Thabo at time 2”, “coin toss by Tumi at time 1”, and “coin toss by Tumi at time 2” and not repeating any of them.

The choice among these interpretations turns on the experimenter’s prior beliefs: Does the likelihood of a head or tail change over time? Does it change based on who tosses the coin? Such considerations are routine in statistical modeling of longitudinal data. A question researchers do not often ask, however, is whether one person’s coin toss affects another’s.

Interdependence

Now consider the experiment “Thabo tosses a coin, then Tumi tosses the coin, then Thabo tosses the coin again, then Tumi tosses the coin again.” Whereas in the above listing we implicitly assumed that each coin toss was self-contained, this sequence accommodates the possibility that the outcomes of Thabo and Tumi’s coin tosses might be causally related.

We can quantitatively assess this possibility by repeating the sequence many times and computing the correlation coefficient between the outcomes of Thabo and Tumi’s tosses. Without explicitly structuring the experiment as a sequence, we have no framework for asking whether their tosses are related.

In epidemiologic practice, the modeling decision to treat individuals or groups as independent is so well-entrenched that it is often presented as a necessary condition for doing valid causal research. Yet with humans—unlike with coins—there is abundant evidence and experience demonstrating that interdependence is the rule rather than the exception.

Towards a causal inference of dependent happenings

Our goal in this appendix and project is to offer ideas and tools for causal inference that allow researchers to learn from interdependence rather than avoid it in pursuit of clear causal reasoning. We can have both clarity and an understanding enriched by the analysis of dependent happenings.

In crude terms, our approach involves moving to a framework where, as a rule, we consider Thabo and Tumi’s coin tosses one experiment. Rather than define a scalar random variable to represent a measure made on \(n\) individuals, we define a vector-valued random variable of length \(n\) and assume that some of its entries are correlated as a result of being part of the same causal structure.

But why do that when our tools for statistical modeling require as many repetitions as possible for valid inferences? How can we quantify uncertainty when we always have a sample size of one?

Regarding the first question: we take this approach because it allows us to investigate and quantify causal interference rather than assume it away.
Regarding the second: SSNAC advocates a shift in perspective.

We re-frame the task of statistical inference from that of learning from a growing collection of identically distributed random vectors to that of learning from a growing random vector whose entries are related through a causal structure. In that causal structure, we distinguish between causal relationships that play out within observational units (“structural”) from those that play out across them (“relational”), making homogeneity assumptions about each.

We avail ourselves of asymptotic theory by making the assumption of no within-covariate influence between observational units—a weaker version of the independence assumption. These assumptions allow our estimates to benefit from having more observational units, even if they collectively contribute to one realization of the statistical model.

In the sections that follow, we describe classic causal inference, social network analysis with causal interpretation, and the SSNAC framework, each time discussing their assumptions, causal model, and causal estimands and illustrating a causal identification strategy. Finally, we describe the main analysis of the report, which applies the SSNAC framework to RESPND-MI data.

C.1 Classical causal inference

Learning goals

  • Write a causal model for a vector of random variables representing measures taken on a group of observational units.

  • Apply the assumptions of FFRCISTG to a causal model for a vector of random variables representing measures taken on a group of observational units.

In the next few sections, we will use Example 1 to describe the assumptions made under classical causal inference. First we give an overview of the ideas and tools we need to make that description: structural equation models and the directed acyclic graph (DAGs) that describe them. We use these to state the basic assumptions of the model currently used to reason about causality in epidemiology - the finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (FFRCISTG). Finally, we derive an FFRCISTG model for this example, showing concrete illustrations for each assumption of the FFRCISTG model.

Example 1: Dress and jobs

We are interested in settling an argument among friends about the relationship between dressing in formal wear and job offers. One friend thinks that dressing formally brings more job offers; everyone she knows who dresses formally has many more job offers than those who dress casually.

The other friend thinks that what really brings job offers is currently having a job. In their reasoning, having a job causes people to dress formally and also causes people to receive job offers, but dressing formally does not in itself improve a person’s likelihood of receiving job offers. We conduct a study to investigate.

We recruit three people, Person 1, Person 2, and Person 3. Person 1 and Person 2 are married to each other. Person 1 and Person 3 are acquaintances.

TOOLKIT A: Causal reasoning under independence
NPSEM

A nonparametric structural equation model (NPSEM) is a system of deterministic equations that relates all the entries in a given random vector to each other. It does so without specifying parametric functions for each equation in the system. The same equations also say how the variables would be generated in hypothetical worlds in which some of them are set by intervention. An example of such a model is shown below. In this set of equations \((f_*)\), values that are produced by equations higher up in the list serve as inputs to equations lower down in the list.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}, L_1) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3}, L_1, L_2) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1, L_2, L_3) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3, Y_1) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3, Y_1, Y_2) \end{aligned} \]

The arguments \((U_*)\) represent error terms—random variables that induce the joint distribution of the variables in the model. In this example every variable depends on all the variables before it, which makes the NPSEM complete. When error terms are mutually independent, within and across observational units, the model is called an NPSEM-IE—a nonparametric structural equation model with independent errors.

Intervention regime

We imagine that in certain types of studies, the investigator is able to “intervene” on an NPSEM by setting the value of a random variable through experiment (a randomized control trial, for instance). In static interventions the value is set to a constant whereas in dynamic interventions the value is set by a rule that depends on variables measured earlier (for example, treat only those whose earlier measurement crossed a threshold). An intervention regime is a collection of interventions.

Causal model

A causal model is a nonparametric structural equation model that mimics the causal process that produced the variables under investigation. This is done with the goal of reasoning about the impact of a given intervention regime. It is a combination of two NPSEMs: a model of the observed data and a model of the interventional data—hypothetical data that would be observed under the given intervention regime. In a causal model, the observational and interventional NPSEMs have identical equations and equal error terms.

Consider a causal model for an intervention regime where we set formal dress \(A_1\) to \(a_1\), \(A_2\) to \(a_2\), and \(A_3\) to \(a_3\). The observed data model would be:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}, L_1) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3}, L_1, L_2) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1, L_2, L_3) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3, Y_1) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3, Y_1, Y_2) \end{aligned} \tag{C.1}\]

In the interventional data model (shown in Equation C.2 below), the values set by the investigator supersede the values that would have been generated had the experimenter not intervened. The experimenter-set values are used in subsequent equations in the model.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}, L_1) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3}, L_1, L_2) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1, L_2, L_3) \\ A_2^{(a_1)} &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_1, L_2, L_3, a_1) \\ A_3^{(a_1, a_2)} &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_1, L_2, L_3, a_1, a_2) \\ Y_1^{(a_1, a_2, a_3)} &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, L_2, L_3, a_1, a_2, a_3) \\ Y_2^{(a_1, a_2, a_3)} &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_1, L_2, L_3, a_1, a_2, a_3, Y_1^{(a_1, a_2, a_3)}) \\ Y_3^{(a_1, a_2, a_3)} &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_1, L_2, L_3, a_1, a_2, a_3, Y_1^{(a_1, a_2, a_3)}, Y_2^{(a_1, a_2, a_3)}) \end{aligned} \tag{C.2}\]

To summarize: a causal model is an NPSEM made up of two NPSEMs. The NPSEMs are the observed data model and the interventional data model. The interventional data model is produced by applying the intervention regime to the observed data model. The error terms of the interventional data model are restricted so that they are equal to those of the observed data model.

DAGs and SWIGs

Under independence assumptions such as those of the NPSEM-IE or the FFRCISTG, an NPSEM implies a set of conditional independencies among its random variables. In principle these can be deduced from the structural equations themselves, by tracing which disturbances and which earlier variables each variable depends on. It turns out that queries like this are much simpler to answer through a graphical representation of the NPSEM than through the NPSEM itself, however. Directed acyclic graphs allow the efficient communication of conditional independence assumptions that define NPSEMs.

A causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Figure C.1) is a graph whose nodes represent the variables in an NPSEM (Equation C.1). Directed edges point from the arguments to the result of each structural equation.

DAGs and SWIGs are useful because they encode statistical relationships among the variables in an NPSEM through a set of graphical rules called d-separation. Under an FFRCISTG, the law of the observed variables factorizes over the DAG as \(p(\mathbf v) = \prod_{V \in \mathbf V} p(v \mid \mathbf{pa}_V)\), so two variables that are d-separated given a set \(\mathbf B\) in the DAG are conditionally independent given \(\mathbf B\). The converse needs an extra assumption (faithfulness). In the SWIG, d-separation gives conditional independencies among the counterfactual variables in the same way.

FFRCISTG

To fully characterize the NPSEM specified above using observed data, we would need to estimate nine functions using one observation of the nine-dimensional joint distribution that gave rise to the participants’ observations. Recruiting an additional person to the study would not ease this problem; it would add three new equations to the model, and we would end up with a similar problem to the one we began with. We would have to estimate 12 functions using one observation of a 12-dimensional joint distribution.

We can simplify this system of equations by making assumptions that leave us with fewer functions to estimate and more observations per function (homogeneity assumptions), and assumptions that simplify the procedure for estimation (independence assumptions).

The finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (FFRCISTG) is a causal model defined by the independence assumptions of: i) no interference, and ii) no within-covariate influence, and the homogeneity assumption of iii) identical distributions, together with the assumption that single-world counterfactuals are mutually independent (Definition C.15). Under (i) and (ii) that independence makes the observational units independent of each other, and under (iii) they are also identically distributed. For a single unit this is Robins’ FFRCISTG.

Where the names come from. The FFRCISTG is due to Robins (1986). Richardson and Robins (2013) state it relative to a DAG, as the assumption that single-world counterfactuals are mutually independent: assumption (4) of Definition C.15, written out in Definition C.4. Assumptions (1)–(3) are not part of Robins’ definition. We add them so that observational units are independent and identically distributed, which is the setting of this chapter.

The network models later in this appendix are not new kinds of model. The NFFRCISTG (Definition C.27) is an FFRCISTG over the DAG whose nodes are every unit’s variables, together with the interference and homogeneity assumptions listed in its definition. The RNFFRCISTG (Definition C.31) and BRNFFRCISTG (Definition C.33) relax it in one respect only: where the network is generated inside the model, single-world independence is required of the structural variables, and relational variables within a graph generator may be dependent. The prefixes N, RN and BRN are our labels for these combinations, as is the name NPSEM-GE (Definition C.29). Neither is standard terminology.

Robins, James. 1986. “A New Approach to Causal Inference in Mortality Studies with a Sustained Exposure Periodapplication to Control of the Healthy Worker Survivor Effect.” Mathematical Modelling 7 (9-12): 13931512.
Formal definitions

Definition C.1 (Random variable) A quantity \(V(\omega)\) that takes a value for each element \(\omega\) of a population \(\Omega\) and behaves according to some (unknown) probability distribution. Its values may be scalars, vectors or matrices.

Definition C.2 (Statistical model) A collection of probability distributions \(\mathcal M\) on a vector of random variables \(\mathbf V\), indexed by a parameter \(\theta\).

\[ \mathcal{M}=\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}\quad\text{on the random variables }\mathbf V. \]

Definition C.3 (Non-parametric structural equation model [NPSEM]) A statistical model defined relative to a directed acyclic graph \(\mathcal G\) on \(\mathbf V\). Each random variable \(V\) is returned by a deterministic function \(f_V\), called a structural equation, of its parents \(\mathbf{Pa}(V)\) in \(\mathcal G\) and an error term \(U_V\), which may be vector-valued:

\[ V = f_V\big(\mathbf{Pa}(V), U_V\big), \qquad V \in \mathbf V. \]

The parameter \(\theta = \big(\{f_V\}_{V\in\mathbf V},\, P_{\mathbf U}\big)\) is the set of structural equations together with the joint law of the error terms. The same equations also give the values the variables would take in hypothetical worlds in which some equations are replaced (interventional NPSEM, causal model). Because \(\mathcal G\) is acyclic, the variables can be put in a topological order \(V_1,\dots,V_m\) in which every parent precedes its children:

\[ V_{j} = f_{V_j}\big(\mathbf{Pa}(V_j),\, U_{V_j}\big), \qquad \mathbf{Pa}(V_j)\subseteq\{V_1,\dots,V_{j-1}\}. \]

The NPSEM is complete if \(\mathbf{Pa}(V_j) = \{V_1,\dots,V_{j-1}\}\) for every \(j\). The definition places no restriction on the joint law of the error terms; such restrictions are separate assumptions.

Definition C.4 (Single-world independence) For every value \(\mathbf v\) of \(\mathbf V\), the counterfactuals

\[ \big\{\, V^{(\mathbf{pa}_V)} : V \in \mathbf V \,\big\} \]

are mutually independent. Here \(\mathbf{pa}_V\) is the sub-vector of \(\mathbf v\) that gives values to the parents of \(V\), and \(V^{(\mathbf{pa}_V)}\) is the value \(V\) would take if its parents were set to \(\mathbf{pa}_V\).

Each variable contributes exactly one counterfactual here — the one in which its parents are held at the values \(\mathbf v\) assigns them — so the condition is about variables within a single hypothetical world, not across several.

A stronger condition in common use requires the error terms themselves to be mutually independent, which says that no two variables share a cause the model leaves out. That implies single-world independence, and the converse does not hold. The two differ only for cross-world quantities, such as natural direct effects, which single-world independence alone does not identify.

This is the independence condition the FFRCISTG assumes.

Definition C.5 (Parent) A random variable \(X\) is a parent of random variable \(Y\) if the directed acyclic graph \(\mathcal G\) has an edge \(X \to Y\). The set of parents of \(Y\) is denoted \(\mathbf{Pa}(Y)\). In an NPSEM the parents of \(Y\) are exactly the arguments of its structural equation, so the missing edges of \(\mathcal G\) are what the model asserts: a variable that is not a parent of \(Y\) has no direct effect on it.

Definition C.6 (Observational unit) A sub-vector \(\mathscr{O}_i\), \(i \in [1,n]\), of the random variables \(\mathbf V\) such that

\[ \mathbf V = \big[ \mathscr{O}_1, \dots, \mathscr{O}_n\big]^T. \]

Definition C.7 (Equivalence and covariates) Two random variables are equivalent if they belong to different observational units but represent the same construct. The vector of all mutually equivalent variables, such as \((Y_1,\dots,Y_n)\), is a covariate.

\[ X_i\sim X_j\iff X_i\in \mathscr{O}_i,\ X_j\in \mathscr{O}_j,\ X_i \text{ and } X_j \text{ represent the same construct},\ i\ne j. \]

Definition C.8 (No within-covariate influence assumption) No variable in a given observational unit is a parent of an equivalent variable in another unit: no element of a covariate influences another element of the same covariate.

\[ X\notin \mathbf{Pa}(X')\quad \forall\, X\sim X',\ X\in \mathscr O_i,\ X'\in \mathscr O_j,\ i\ne j. \]

Definition C.9 (No interference assumption) No variable in a given observational unit is a parent of a non-equivalent variable in another unit.

\[ X\notin \mathbf{Pa}(Y)\quad \forall\, X\in \mathscr O_i,\ Y\in \mathscr O_j,\ X\not\sim Y,\ i\ne j. \]

This assumption and no within-covariate influence together are the no-interference content of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA).

Definition C.10 (Identical distributions assumption) Equivalent variables have identical structural equations and identically distributed error terms.

\[ X_i\sim X_j\Rightarrow f_{X_i}=f_{X_j}\ \text{ and }\ U_{X_i}\overset{d}{=}U_{X_j}. \]

Definition C.11 (Intervention equation) An intervention equation for random variable \(V_j\) is a deterministic function \(h_{V_j}\) that returns a value in the range of \(V_j\) from (a sub-vector of) the variables that precede \(V_j\) in the topological order of the NPSEM. It does not take the error term \(U_{V_j}\) as an input. The intervention is static if \(h_{V_j}\) is constant and dynamic otherwise.

Definition C.12 (Intervention regime) A set of intervention equations defined on a subset of random variables \(\mathbf E \subset \mathbf V\).

\[ \Gamma = \{ h_V \}_{V \in \mathbf{E}} \]

Definition C.13 (Interventional NPSEM) The NPSEM obtained from \(\mathcal{M}\) by replacing the structural equations of the variables in \(\mathbf E\) with the intervention equations of intervention regime \(\Gamma\).

\[ \mathcal{M}^{(\Gamma)} = \{f_V^{(\Gamma)}: V \in \mathbf V \} \qquad f_V^{(\Gamma)} = \begin{cases} h_V & \text{if } V \in \mathbf E\\ f_V & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]

Definition C.14 (Causal model) A causal model \(\mathcal C\) of an “observational” NPSEM \(\mathcal M\) under intervention regime \(\Gamma\) combines \(\mathcal M\) with its interventional NPSEM \(\mathcal M^{(\Gamma)}\), constraining the error term of each variable in \(\mathcal M^{(\Gamma)}\) to equal the error term of the same variable in \(\mathcal M\).

\[ \mathcal{C} = \mathcal{M} \cup \mathcal M^{(\Gamma)} \qquad \text{for all } V \in \mathbf V, \quad U_V = U^{(\Gamma)}_V \]

The counterfactuals are the variables of \(\mathcal M^{(\Gamma)}\): they are computed by recursive substitution through the same structural equations and the same error terms as the observed variables. A causal model is a structure, not yet a causal claim; what turns it into an FFRCISTG is the further assumptions that model makes.

Definition C.15 (FFRCISTG model) A finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (FFRCISTG) is a causal model of an NPSEM \(\mathcal M\), relative to DAG \(\mathcal G\), under intervention regime \(\Gamma\), in which \(\mathcal M\) satisfies (1) no within-covariate influence, (2) no interference, (3) identical distributions, and (4) single-world independence.

Assumption (4) is weaker than assuming the error terms mutually independent, and the difference matters only for cross-world quantities, such as natural direct effects, which the FFRCISTG does not identify.

D-separation cheat sheet

D-separation is a set of rules for translating DAGs into conditional independence statements. A pair of nodes is d-separated if all the paths linking them are closed or if there is no path that links them. A pair of nodes is conditionally d-separated if all the paths linking them are closed after conditioning on some set of nodes.

A path is a sequence of nodes that starts with an origin node and ends with a terminal node. Consecutive entries in the sequence are connected by an edge in the DAG, pointing in either direction, and no node appears twice. A path of length one is open by definition. A path of length two or more is open if all its triangles are open.

A triangle is a sub-sequence of a path which contains three consecutive nodes from the path. A canonical triangle is one in which we do not condition on the apex node. A conditional triangle is one in which we do condition.

C.1.1 Assumptions

In Example 1, say Person 1 has job status \(L_1\), Person 2 has job status \(L_2\) and Person 3 has job status \(L_3\). Each of these variables can only take on two values: “employed vs. unemployed.” Similarly, define \(A_i\) as Person \(i\)’s dress (formal vs. informal), and \(Y_i\) as their job offers (many vs. few). The job status measurement (\(L_i\)) precedes dress (\(A_i\)) in time, and dress precedes job offers (\(Y_i\)).

Independence I: No within-covariate influence

Under classic causal inference, we make the assumption of no within-covariate influence – that equivalent measurements taken across participants (e.g. \(L_1\), \(L_2\), and \(L_3\)) are independent of each other given all prior measures. i.e.:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 \perp L_2 \\ L_2 \perp L_3 \\ L_1 \perp L_3 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} A_1 \perp A_2 | L_1, L_2, L_3\\ A_2 \perp A_3 | L_1, L_2, L_3 \\ A_1 \perp A_3 | L_1, L_2, L_3 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_1 \perp Y_2 | L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3\\ Y_2 \perp Y_3 | L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3 \\ Y_1 \perp Y_3 | L_1, L_2, L_3, A_1, A_2, A_3 \end{aligned} \]

This is shown in Figure C.3 as the removal of arrows that connected the \(L_i\)’s, \(A_i\)’s and \(Y_i\)’s. In Equation C.3, the \(L_i\)’s are not functions of each other, the \(A_i\)’s are not functions of each other, and the \(Y_i\)’s are not functions of each other. i.e. Given all the measurements taken prior to a set of contemporaneous measures, the contemporaneous measures are independent. This follows from the independence of the error terms \(U_{L_i}\), the error terms \(U_{A_i}\), and the error terms \(U_{Y_i}\) across observations \(i\) (or, more generally, from the independence assumption (4) of the FFRCISTG).

Independence II: No interference

Classic causal inference makes the assumption of no interference. i.e. We assume that measurements made across participants, but not at the same time, are independent of each other. This is shown as the absence of arrows that point from the \(L_i\)’s to \(A_j\)’s, the \(L_i\)’s to the \(Y_j\)’s and the \(A_i\)’s to the \(Y_j\) for participants \(i\neq j\) (see Figure C.4).

This allows us to simplify the structural equation model so that each participant’s measures are a function of only measures taken on them.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3} ) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_2) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_3) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, A_1, ) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_2, A_2) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_3, A_3) \\ \end{aligned} \tag{C.4}\]

Homogeneity I: Identical distributions

Finally, we see above that the functions that generate observations \(L_i\) are similar in structure to each other, as are the functions that generate \(A_i\)’s and \(Y_i\)s. We make the homogeneity assumption that the functions are identical to each other.

\[ \begin{aligned} f_L &= f_{L_1} = f_{L_2} = f_{L_3} \\ f_A &= f_{A_1} = f_{A_2} = f_{A_3} \\ f_Y &= f_{Y_1} = f_{Y_2} = f_{Y_3} \\ \end{aligned} \]

C.1.4 Identification

Once we have specified the hypothetical quantities from our thought experiment, we “identify” them, meaning write them in terms of the observed rather than counterfactual model. It is sufficient to identify the quantity:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[Y_i^{(a)}] &= E \left[ E[Y_i^{(a)} | L_i] \right] \\ &= E \left[ E[f_{Y}(U_{Y_i} , L_i, a) | L_i] \right] \\ &= E \left[ E[f_{Y}(U_{Y_i} , L_i, a) | L_i , A_i] \right] \\ &= E \left[ E[f_{Y}(U_{Y_i} , L_i, a) | L_i , A_i = a] \right] \\ &= E \left[ E[Y_i | L_i , A_i = a] \right] \\ \end{aligned} \]

In the first line, we use the law of iterated expectations. In the second line, we use the definition for counterfactual outcome \(Y_i^{(a)}\) as specified in Equation C.6. In the third line, we use the independence assumption (4) of the FFRCISTG. Fix \(L_i = l\). Then \(f_{Y}(U_{Y_i}, l, a) = Y_i^{(l, a)}\) and \(A_i = A_i^{(l)}\), and (4) says that \(L_i\), \(A_i^{(l)}\) and \(Y_i^{(l, a)}\) are mutually independent. Hence \(f_{Y}(U_{Y_i} , L_i, a) \perp A_i | L_i\). (Under an NPSEM-IE the same conclusion follows directly from the independence of \(U_{L_i}\), \(U_{A_i}\) and \(U_{Y_i}\).) In the fourth line we consider the expected value of \(f_{Y}(U_{Y_i} , L_i, a)\) when \(A_i = a\), in particular; positivity ensures this event has positive probability in every stratum of \(L_i\). In the fifth line, we use the definition for observed value \(Y_i\) as specified in Equation C.5.

C.1.5 Remarks

This section illustrated the model assumptions of the FFRCISTG—the foundation of modern causal inference in epidemiology—namely, independence and identical distributions. We decomposed the assumption of independence into two components: no within-covariate influence and no interference. We showed that the assumption of identical distributions is a statement about the structural equations that govern the causal process as it plays out within each observational unit.

Students of causal inference might be surprised that the identification assumptions—exchangeability and consistency—are not mentioned explicitly. That is because, within the FFRCISTG framework, these assumptions are not external requirements but direct consequences of the model itself.

Consistency follows from the equality of the structural functions and error terms that define counterfactual and observed outcomes within a causal model. Exchangeability, in turn, arises from the independence of single-world counterfactuals (assumption (4) of Definition C.15) and the recursive structure of the NPSEM. No within-covariate influence and no interference together are the no-interference part of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA).

In the next section, we relax the assumption of no interference, introduce homogeneity assumptions about the process of interference, and arrive at a model called the Network FFRCISTG, which allows us to reason about causality in the presence of a fixed pattern of interference.

Example 2: Marriage and jobs

We mentioned that in our study sample Person 1 and Person 2 are married to each other and Person 1 and Person 3 are acquaintances. In the causal system connecting their job status, dress, and job offers, an argument could be made that Person 1’s job status affects Person 2’s dress, since they pool their salaries as a married couple. It could also be argued that Person 1’s job status affects Person 2’s job opportunities if having a job brings the married couple into contact with other married couples who are connected to job markets.

Finally, we could argue that Person 1’s dress affects Person 2’s job opportunities if some aspect of the reputation Person 2 enjoys among potential colleagues derives from their spouse’s appearance. Symmetrically, Person 2’s measures could influence Person 1’s.

But what about the relationship between Person 1 and Person 3? We can imagine that the dress of a newly acquainted colleague would not affect the dress or job opportunities of her colleague. Whereas Person 1 and Person 2 are causally related, Person 1 and Person 3 are not; their relationship is irrelevant to the causal system under investigation. Person 2 and Person 3 do not affect each other since they have no relationship at all.

Because of the assumptions of no interference and no within-covariate influence, FFRCISTG models are unable to incorporate this additional information about the relationships between individuals in Example 2. These relationships cause what is termed causal interference — the treatment level of one unit affects the outcome of another unit.

We make progress by mapping out the structure of interference using graphs and incorporate it into the model. We introduce a statistical model called the Network Finest Fully Randomized Causally Interpretable Structured Tree Graph (NFFRCISTG) — an extension of the FFRCISTG model that uses graphs to encode the pattern of causal interference among a set of observational units.

Toolkit B: Causal reasoning under network interference
Graphs

A graph is a set of elements along with a rule describing whether they are connected or not. Whereas structural equations describe causal structure within observational units, graphs provide a language for describing causal structure across units. A graph represents units as nodes and possible pathways of influence between units as edges. They help us represent who is eligible to affect whom. When two nodes are connected by an edge, we call them neighbors.

Network exposure mappings

While graphs tell us who is connected, we need a way to incorporate the information they contain into the structural equations that define the variables of the model. Network exposure mappings do this by turning the vector of neighbors’ treatments into a scalar quantity — for example, the count or proportion of treated neighbors. Together with graphs, exposure mappings formalize causal interference: graphs define the structure of possible influence across units, while network exposure mappings define how each unit incorporates that influence in its own measures.

The term exposure mapping is Aronow and Samii’s (2017). In their framework it is a function from the full vector of treatment assignments to the exposure each unit receives, and it is assumed to be correctly specified, so that a unit’s potential outcomes depend on the assignment only through its exposure. The idea is close to Manski’s effective treatments (2013). We use the term in two ways that differ from theirs. First, the mapping may summarize any covariate that contains a parent of the outcome, not only treatment. Second, the mapping itself is only notation (Definition C.20): the substantive content sits in the assumptions placed on it, separability, positional invariance and boundedness. This follows Sävje’s (2024) argument for separating the definition of exposures from assumptions about the structure of interference.

Sävje, Fredrik. 2024. “Causal Inference with Misspecified Exposure Mappings: Separating Definitions and Assumptions.” Biometrika 111 (1): 115. https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/asad019.
Manski, Charles F. 2013. “Identification of Treatment Response with Social Interactions.” The Econometrics Journal 16 (1): S1S23. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1368-423X.2012.00368.x.

Homogeneous network interference

The assumption of homogeneous network interference holds that for any node in the network, the effect of a neighbor’s treatment is the same regardless of which neighbor it is, and regardless of the position of the unit in the network. In addition, network exposure mappings are restricted so that their values lie in a finite set.

Network FFRCISTG

The network finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (NFFRCISTG) is a causal model defined by the independence assumptions of: i) networked interference, and ii) no within-covariate influence, and the homogeneity assumptions of iii) homogeneous network interference, and iv) identical distributions.

Formal definitions

Definition C.16 (Graph) An ordered pair \(\mathscr{G} = (\mathcal{N}, \psi)\) with node set \(\mathcal{N}\) and adjacency function \(\psi\). The adjacency function assigns a nonnegative weight to each unordered pair of nodes \((i,j)\).

\[ \mathscr G=(\mathcal N,\psi),\qquad \psi:\mathcal N\times\mathcal N\to \mathbb R_{\ge 0},\qquad \psi(i,j)=\psi(j,i) \]

Nodes \(i,j \in \mathcal{N}\) are connected if and only if \(\psi(i,j) > 0\).

The nodes of a graph are observational units, not random variables, and its edges carry no direction: it represents which units may influence which. The directed acyclic graph of an NPSEM is a different object, over variables.

Definition C.17 (Neighborhood) The set of nodes connected to a given node \(i \in \mathcal{N}\) in graph \(\mathscr{G} = (\mathcal{N}, \psi)\).

\[ \mathcal{N}_i = \{ j \in \mathcal{N} : \psi(i,j) > 0 \}. \]

Definition C.18 (Network weights) The vector of the edge weights node \(i\) shares with the other nodes of graph \(\mathscr{G} = (\mathcal{N}, \psi)\).

\[ \Psi_i = [\psi(i,1), \dots, \psi(i,n)]^T \qquad n=|\mathcal N|. \]

Both the full vector and its restriction to the neighborhood, written \(\Psi_{i,\mathcal N_i}\), are used. The restriction, which drops exactly the zero entries, is what positional invariance compares across nodes; the full vector is what the identification formulas pass to an exposure mapping once that assumption lets a single function serve every unit.

Definition C.19 (Network values) The sub-vector of some \(\mathbf X \in \{0,1\}^n\) whose entries belong to nodes in the neighborhood of \(i\) in graph \(\mathscr{G} = (\mathcal{N}, \psi)\).

\[ \mathbf X_{\mathcal N_i}=\big(X_j\big)_{j\in \mathcal N_i} \]

Definition C.20 (Exposure mapping) A function \(g_i : \{0,1\}^{K \times n} \to \mathbb{R}^m\) that summarizes, for unit \(i\), the covariates containing the parents of \(Y_i\), so that the structural equation can be written

\[ Y_i = f_{Y_i}\big(g_i(\mathbf{X}_1,\dots,\mathbf{X}_K), U_{Y_i}\big), \]

where \(f_{Y_i}\) is the structural equation for \(Y_i\) in the NPSEM and \(\mathbf X_1,\dots,\mathbf X_K\) are the covariates that contain the parents of \(Y_i\).

Writing an equation this way is a change of notation, not a claim: an unrestricted \(g_i\) says no more than the NPSEM does, and could be the identity. What makes exposure mappings useful are the assumptions placed on them — separability, positional invariance and boundedness — which is also where graphs enter.

Definition C.21 (Separability) An exposure mapping \(g_i\) can be written as the unit’s own values alongside one summary per covariate:

\[ g_i(\mathbf{X}_1,\dots,\mathbf{X}_K) = \Big[ \begin{pmatrix} X_{1i} \\ \vdots \\ X_{Ki} \end{pmatrix},\, g_{1i}(\mathbf{X}_1),\, \dots,\, g_{Ki}(\mathbf{X}_K)\Big]^T, \]

where \(X_{ki} \in \mathscr O_i\) is the \(i^{th}\) entry of \(\mathbf X_k\) and each \(g_{ti} : \{0,1\}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) is a component function of \(g_i\). A structural equation is separable if its exposure mapping is. Separability is what lets influence from other units arrive through a few summaries rather than through all \(n-1\) of their values.

Definition C.22 (Loyalty) A component function \(g_{ti}\) is loyal to graph \(\mathscr{G}\) if for all \(\mathbf{X}_t,\mathbf{X}'_t \in \{0,1\}^n\)

\[ \mathbf{X}_{t\mathcal{N}_i} = \mathbf{X}'_{t\mathcal{N}_i} \implies g_{ti}(\mathbf{X}_t) = g_{ti}( \mathbf{X}'_t). \]

A structural equation is loyal to graph \(\mathscr{G}\) if it is separable and all its component functions are loyal to \(\mathscr{G}\). Loyalty is implied by positional invariance, taking \(j=i\) and \(\mathbf M = \mathbf I\).

Definition C.23 (Positional invariance) The value of a component function is unchanged by a re-ordering of network values together with the same re-ordering of network weights. For all \(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}' \in \{0,1\}^n\) and every permutation matrix \(\mathbf M\),

\[ \mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{N}_i} = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}'_{\mathcal{N}_j}, \quad \Psi_{i,\mathcal{N}_i} = \mathbf{M}\Psi_{j,\mathcal{N}_j} \Rightarrow g_{ti}(\mathbf{X}) = g_{tj}(\mathbf{X}'). \]

An exposure mapping is positionally invariant with respect to a graph set \(\mathcal H = \{\mathscr{G}_t\}_{t \in [1:K]}\) if it is separable and each component function is positionally invariant with respect to an element of \(\mathcal H\). Two nodes in the same local configuration are summarized the same way, whoever they are and wherever they sit.

This is what makes the notation of the identification formulas legitimate. Without it there are \(n\) separate component functions \(g_{t1},\dots,g_{tn}\), one per unit; positional invariance collapses them into a single function of the values and the node’s position, so that one \(g_Y(\mathbf L, \Psi_i)\) can stand for every \(i\).

Definition C.24 (Boundedness) A component function takes values in a finite set: there is a finite \(\mathcal{D}\) such that \(g_{ti}(\mathbf{X}) \in \mathcal{D}\) for all \(\mathbf{X} \in \{0,1\}^n\). An exposure mapping is bounded if it is separable and all its component functions are bounded.

Boundedness is a homogeneity assumption because it is what makes exposure comparable across units: finitely many exposure levels, each occupied by many units, is what allows one function to be estimated from the whole population.

Definition C.25 (Network exposure mapping) A component function that is bounded and positionally invariant with respect to some graph \(\mathscr{G}_t\). A name for those two assumptions together, nothing more.

Definition C.26 (Homogeneous network interference) An exposure mapping is separable and all its component functions are network exposure mappings with respect to a graph set \(\mathcal H\). An NPSEM satisfies homogeneous network interference if all its exposure mappings do.

The name is the decomposition. Interference arrives through separable summaries; it is homogeneous because those summaries treat every node alike (positional invariance) and take finitely many levels (boundedness). When \(\mathcal H\) holds graph generators rather than fixed graphs, the same assumption is called homogeneous random network interference.

Definition C.27 (NFFRCISTG model) A network finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (NFFRCISTG) is a causal model of an NPSEM \(\mathcal M\) under intervention regime \(\Gamma\), in which \(\mathcal M\) satisfies (1) no within-covariate influence, (2) homogeneous network interference with respect to some graph set \(\mathcal H\), (3) identical distributions, and (4) the single-world independence of the FFRCISTG.

Interference is carried by exposure mappings, so each unit’s equation has a fixed number of arguments however large \(n\) is, and the average total, direct and spillover effects are identified given positivity. The graph set \(\mathcal H\) is fixed and known: nothing in this model treats the network as uncertain or as measured with error.

Note on boundedness

The assumption of boundedness can obtain in three different ways. It could be as a result of restrictions on the structure of the graph. For example, if the only relationships of interest between individual participants were marriages, each node would have a degree of either 0 or 1, meaning the count of alters with \(X_{tj} = 1, j \in \mathscr{N}_i\) is either zero or one.

It might obtain via restrictions on the value of \(\mathbf{X}\) itself. It might be the case, for instance, that the count of \(i\)’s for which \(X_{ti}=1\) is very low so that on average, the count of alters for which \(X_{tj} = 1, j \in N_i\) will also be low.

Finally, boundedness could obtain as a result of the functional form of \(g\) itself. This might happen if \(g\) models a network exposure which has a ceiling effect in its dose-response curve with the outcome of interest. For example, if \(X\) indicates ownership of a car, and \(\mathscr{G}\) encodes close friendships, \(g\) would be a network exposure mapping which tells us how each additional friend who has a car shifts the outcome of interest. For most health applications, its likely that beyond some number of friends who have a car, an additional friend who gets a car will not change the outcome.

This conditional independence can be checked in data. For household mortality there was no evidence of dependence. A test of this kind checks the conditional independence the assumption implies. It does not tell within-covariate influence apart from an unmeasured common cause of the two measures, which is how that paper reads a rejection.

We make the assumption that interference respects some mapping between individuals - a mapping given by a graph adjacency matrix \(\Psi\). In Figure C.6, based on their relationships with one another, Person 1’s measures are connected with Person 2’s, and neither are connected with Person 3’s.

\[ \Psi = \begin{bmatrix} 0,& 1,& 0 \\ 1,& 0,& 0 \\ 0,& 0,& 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} \]

Homogeneity I: Homogeneous network interference

We assume that the exposure mapping for each variable in the structural model is separable. i.e. We define \(g_{A_i}\), \(h_{Y_i}\), and \(g_{Y_i}\) as component functions for \(A_i\) with respect to \(\mathbf{L}\), for \(Y_i\) with respect to \(\mathbf{A}\), and for \(Y_i\) with respect to \(\mathbf{L}\). The assumption of separability allows us to write the model as follows:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3} ) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1, g_{A_1}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_1)) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_2, g_{A_2}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_2)) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_3, g_{A_3}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_3)) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, A_1, g_{Y_1}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_1), h_{Y_1}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_1)) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_2, A_2, g_{Y_2}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_2), h_{Y_2}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_2)) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_3,A_3, g_{Y_3}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_3), h_{Y_3}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_3)) \\ \end{aligned} \]

where \(\Psi_i\) is the \(i^{th}\) row of matrix \(\Psi\).

We assume that the value of a component function is invariant to its position in the network. This allows us to drop the person subscript on the network exposure mappings.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3} ) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1, g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_1)) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_2, g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_2)) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_3, g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_3)) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, A_1, g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_1), h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_1)) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_2, A_2, g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_2), h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_2)) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_3,A_3, g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_3), h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_3)) \\ \end{aligned} \]

We also assume its bounded, meaning

\[ \begin{aligned} g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_i) &\in \{0, 1\} \\ g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_i) &\in \{0, 1\} \\ h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_i) &\in \{0, 1\} \\ \end{aligned} \]

Homogeneity II: Identical distributions

We assume that the structural equations that produce individual measures are identical across individuals. Hence, we can drop the person subscript from the structural equations.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L}(U_{L_2}) \\ L_3 &= f_{L}(U_{L_3} ) \\ A_1 &= f_{A}(U_{A_1}, L_1, g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_1)) \\ A_2 &= f_{A}(U_{A_2}, L_2, g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_2)) \\ A_3 &= f_{A}(U_{A_3}, L_3, g_{A}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_3)) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, A_1, g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_1), h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_1)) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y}(U_{Y_2}, L_2, A_2, g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_2), h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_2)) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y}(U_{Y_3}, L_3,A_3, g_{Y}(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_3), h_{Y}(\mathbf{A}, \Psi_3)) \\ \end{aligned} \]

C.2.3 Estimands

We are interested in whether on average, changing someone’s dress would change their level of job offers. To determine the answer, we will compare the average counterfactual outcome of job opportunities if everyone were forced to wear formal dress to the average counterfactual outcome of job opportunities if everyone wore non-formal dress. i.e. We will make the following comparison:

\[ ATE = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i E[Y_i^{(a = 1, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i} = \textbf{ 1})} - Y_i^{(a = 0, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i} = \textbf{ 0})}] \] It is possible to decompose this average causal effect into a portion attributable to direct (within individual) causal pathways. The decomposition mirrors the direct, indirect and total effects that Hudgens and Halloran (2008) defined under interference. Theirs compare treatment allocation strategies across groups; these compare fixed treatments of a unit and its neighbors. We call the indirect component a spillover effect. Makofane et al. (2023) estimate the same three quantities for the effect of own and family wealth on physical function. This tells us, on average, to what extent one’s own dress affects one own’s job opportunities.

C.2.4 Identification

\[ \begin{aligned} E[Y_i^{(a_i, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i})} ] &= E[E[Y_i^{(a_i, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i})} | \mathbf{L}]] \\ &= E[E[Y_i^{(a_i, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i})} | L_i , g_Y(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_i)] ] \\ &= E[E[f_{Y}(U_{Y_i}, L_i, a_i, g_{Y}(\textbf{L}, \Psi_i), h_{Y}(\textbf{a}, \Psi_i)) | L_i , g_Y(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_i)] ] \\ &= E[E[f_{Y}(...) | L_i , g_Y(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_i), A_i = a_i, h_Y(\textbf{A}, \Psi_i) = h_Y(\textbf{a}, \Psi_i)]] \\ &= E[E[Y_i | L_i , g_Y(\mathbf{L}, \Psi_i), A_i = a_i, h_Y(\textbf{A}, \Psi_i) = h_Y(\textbf{a}, \Psi_i)]] \\ \end{aligned} \]

In the first line we use the law of iterated expectations. In the second line, we use the separability of the structural equation for \(Y_i^{(a_i, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i})}\). In the third line we replace substitute for the structural equation. In the fourth line, we use the independence assumption (4) of the NFFRCISTG, which makes the single-world counterfactuals of \(Y_i\) independent of \((\mathbf L, \mathbf A)\), so that \(\mathbf{A}\) is independent of \(f_{Y}(...)\) given \(L_i\) and \(g_Y(\mathbf L, \Psi_i)\); positivity ensures the conditioning event has positive probability. In the final line we use the fact that, in the causal model, the structural equation for \(Y_i^{(a_i, \mathbf{a}_{\mathscr{N}_i})}\) in the interventional NPSEM is identical to the structural equation for \(Y_i\) in the observed data NPSEM, with the same error term (consistency).

C.2.5 Remarks

This section extended the classical framework by relaxing the assumption of independence across observational units. By introducing homogeneous network interference, we allowed observational units to affect one another through a fixed, structured pattern of connections, while assuming that the mechanism of influence is identical across all relationships.

Homogeneous network interference makes it possible to represent interference formally and to study its causal implications analytically. Under this assumption, we can summarize the influence of the network on each unit through exposure mappings, which translate complex interdependence into measurable quantities that can be modeled within standard causal frameworks.

This homogeneous interference model -— the Network FFRCISTG —- preserves the tractability of the classical FFRCISTG while acknowledging the presence of spillover effects. In the next section, we generalize further to consider random network interference, in which the network itself is treated as a random structure rather than a fixed pattern of relationships.

C.3 Social-spatial networks

Learning goals

  • Use graph generators to represent arbitrary and random patterns of mutual influence among a group of observational units with two classes: people and places.

  • Apply the assumptions of BRNFFRCISTG to a causal model including graph generators.

A drawback of the NFFRCISTG model described in Section C.2 is that it assumes patterns of causal interference are known. As a consequence, it is not possible to acknowledge the inherent uncertainty in our understanding of the network and, moreso, our measurement of it.

In this section of the appendix, we extend the NFFRCISTG model by allowing for the inclusion of two classes of observational unit — people and places — and by allowing the relationships between people and places to be randomly generated by the causal model. We name this the Bipartite Random Network FFRCISTG model (BRNFFRCISTG). It is a causal model that includes one or more graph generators — groups of structural equations that model the network edges as random variables.

Before outlining the BRNFFRCISTG, we introduce new tools – the causal directed acyclic network graph (DANG), the single world intervention network graph (SWING), and the NPSEM-GE.

DANGs

We introduce the causal directed acyclic network graph (DANG). A causal DANG encodes a process which exhibits a fixed or random pattern of causal interference across observational units. It represents the process in two parts: the structural part represents the causal process as it unfolds within each unit. The relational part represents the pattern or patterns of influence across units.

The DANG is inspired by the chain graph representation of network data used in auto-g-computation (Tchetgen Tchetgen, Fulcher, and Shpitser 2020; for chain graphs generally, see Lauritzen and Richardson 2002). It is not a chain graph. In a chain graph, undirected edges join variables. In a DANG they join observational units, in the relational part, while the structural part is a DAG over vectors of variables.

The structural part of a DANG is a DAG representing the relationships among random vectors (rather than random scalar variables). It has standard interpretation under d-separation rules rules. The relational part is a Markov random field. Each node represents an observational unit and undirected edges represent mutual causal influence. Observational units admit the Markov property: conditional on neighboring units, a given unit is independent of others in the graph.

NPSEM-GE and graph generators

A graph generator is an array of structural equations, each modelling an edge in a random graph. The error terms included in a graph generator are not independent of each other, but are independent of error terms not included. We define a non-parametric structural equation model that includes graph generators as a non-parametric structural equation model with grouped errors (NPSEM-GE).

Random Network FFRCISTG

The random network finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (RNFFRCISTG) is a causal model defined by the independence assumptions of: i) random network interference, and ii) no within-covariate influence, and the homogeneity assumptions of iii) homogeneous random network interference with respect to the included random graphs, iv) identical distributions.

Bipartite Random Network FFRCISTG

A bipartite graph is one where nodes are partitioned into two classes and edges connect only across classes. A bipartite RNFFRCISTG is an RNFFRCISTG in which all graph generators produce bipartite graphs with the same partitioning.

Formal definitions

Definition C.28 (Graph generator) A graph generator gives the entries of a random graph with \(n\) nodes. It is an \(n \times n\) array of structural equations \([f_{\psi_{ij}}]_{i,j \in \mathcal N}\) whose entries \(\psi(i,j)\) are each a deterministic function of other variables in the model and an error term:

\[ \psi(i,j) = f_{\psi_{ij}}\big(\mathbf{Pa}(\psi(i,j)), U_{\psi_{ij}}\big). \]

Because a graph has \(\psi(i,j)=\psi(j,i)\), the array is assumed symmetric in its indices: \(U_{\psi_{ij}} = U_{\psi_{ji}}\) and \(f_{\psi_{ij}} = f_{\psi_{ji}}\).

Definition C.29 (NPSEM-GE) A name for an NPSEM whose equations are of two kinds — structural equations, describing the causal process within units, and relational equations, describing it across units, which are the equations of a graph generator — and whose error terms satisfy two conditions. First, single-world independence holds over the structural variables, which are jointly independent of the relational error terms. Second, relational error terms may be dependent within a generator.

In the bipartite model the group is one person’s row of the adjacency matrix: their attachments to the \(q\) places may share disturbances, while rows are independent across people.

The first condition is what identification rests on. The second restricts nothing — an NPSEM places no restriction on the joint law of its error terms to begin with — so what it records is where dependence is allowed to remain, and therefore the unit of resampling: because dependence is confined within a row, the bootstrap resamples people, not edges.

The NPSEM-GE is therefore not a kind of model of its own. It is a name for an NPSEM with graph generators and one independence condition, used so that the models below can be stated in a line.

Definition C.30 (Homogeneous random network interference) An exposure mapping exhibits homogeneous random network interference with respect to a set of graph generators

\[ \mathcal H = \{\boldsymbol \psi : \boldsymbol \psi \subset \mathbf V \text{ is a graph generator} \} \]

if it is separable and all its component functions are network exposure mappings with respect to \(\mathcal H\). An NPSEM exhibits homogeneous random network interference if all its exposure mappings do. This is homogeneous network interference with \(\mathcal H\) holding graph generators in place of fixed graphs.

Definition C.31 (RNFFRCISTG model) A random network finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (RNFFRCISTG) is a causal model of an NPSEM-GE that satisfies (1) no within-covariate influence, (2) homogeneous random network interference with respect to graph generators \(\mathcal H\), (3) identical distributions, and (4) single-world independence over the structural variables, which are also assumed jointly independent of the generators’ error terms.

Clause (4) is the FFRCISTG condition restricted to the structural variables, which is what identification requires. It does not extend to the relational variables, whose error terms are dependent within a generator by construction.

Because the network is generated inside the model rather than supplied to it, uncertainty about the network is representable, and regimes that act on the network can be considered alongside regimes that act on treatment. Without further structure, however, little is learnable from a single realization of one graph.

Definition C.32 (Bipartite NPSEM-GE) Every graph generator in the model produces bipartite graphs on the same partition of the nodes, for example people and places. An NPSEM-GE satisfying this is a bipartite NPSEM-GE.

Definition C.33 (BRNFFRCISTG model) A bipartite random network finest fully randomized causally interpretable structured tree graph (BRNFFRCISTG) is a causal model of a bipartite NPSEM-GE that satisfies (1) no within-covariate influence, (2) homogeneous random network interference, (3) identical distributions, and (4) single-world independence over the structural variables, as in the RNFFRCISTG.

Units attach to a shared set of places, so repeated attachments do the work that repeated units do under no interference, and the effects of place-level regimes — where to vaccinate, say — are identified given positivity. Regimes acting directly on person-to-person ties are not reached by this model.

Supporting definitions

Two definitions used throughout the appendix: the directed acyclic graph, from Definition C.3 onward, and positivity, in each identification argument.

Definition C.34 (Directed acyclic graph) A graph \(\mathcal G\) whose nodes are the random variables \(\mathbf V\) and whose edges are directed, written \(X \to Y\), with no directed cycle: no sequence of edges leads from a variable back to itself. Acyclicity means the variables can be put in a topological order in which every edge points forwards. Topological orders are not unique, and they need not be chronological.

A directed acyclic graph carries the causal structure among the variables of a model. It is a different object from the weighted, undirected graph used for networks; where both appear, \(\mathcal G\) is the DAG and \(\mathscr G\) the network.

Definition C.35 (Positivity) For a static regime that sets treatment \(A_i\) to \(a\), every treatment level intervened on occurs in every stratum of the conditioning variables that occurs:

\[ P[A_i = a \mid L_i = l] > 0 \quad \text{for every } l \text{ with } P[L_i = l] > 0. \]

Under network interference the event is own treatment together with network exposure, and the strata are those of the variables the identification formula conditions on. Positivity is a condition on the observed data, and can in principle be checked against it.

C.3.1 Assumptions

In Example 3, let \(\mathbf R_{s}\) be the random vector for the risk ratings of each neighborhood at time \(s\); \(\mathbf T_{s}\) be a random vector whose entries indicate if each neighborhood received a vaccination intervention or not based on the rating, \(\mathbf M_{s}\) be a random vector indicating whether each person received a vaccination message through their dating app, and let \(\mathbf V_{s}\) be a random indicator indicating whether each person is vaccinated or not. Each of these vectors is of length \(n\).

Finally, let \(\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}\) be an \(n \times n\) adjacency matrix for the network at time \(s\). We denote arrays of structural equations using upper-case \(F\) and single structural equations using lower-case \(f\). We start with a fully connected NPSEM-GE for random vectors \(\mathbf R_{s}\), \(\mathbf T_{s}\), \(\mathbf M_{s}\), and, \(\mathbf R_{s}\); and graph generator \(\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}\).

Independence 1: No within-covariate influence

We assume no within-covariate influence: equivalent measurements on observational units are independent of each other given all prior measures. This is implied by the following DANG-like compact representation of the model.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Structural } (\mathbb{R}^n) \\ & \mathbf R_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf R_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf R_{s}}, \mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) \\ & \mathbf T_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf T_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf T_{s}}, \mathbf R_{s}) \\ & \mathbf M_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf M_s} (\mathbf U_{\mathbf M_{s}} , \mathbf V_{s-1}, \mathbf M_{s-1}, \mathbf{T}_{s}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s}) \\ & \mathbf V_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf V_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf V_{s}}, \mathbf M_{s}, \mathbf V_{s-1}) \\ \text{Relational } (\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}) \\ & \mathbf{\Psi}_{s} & &= F_{\mathbf{\Psi_s}}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}}, \mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) \\ \end{aligned} \tag{C.10}\]

where

\[ \mathbf f_{*}(\mathbf{U}_*, \mathbf{Pa(*)}) = \begin{bmatrix} f_{*_1}(U_{*_1}, \mathbf{Pa}(*_1))\\ f_{*_2}(U_{*_2}, \mathbf{Pa}(*_2))\\ ... \\ f_{*_n}(U_{*_n}, \mathbf{Pa}(*_n))\\ \end{bmatrix} \]

Independence 2: Random network interference

We make the assumption that interference respects a mapping between individuals and neighborhoods as given by random graph \(\mathbf \Psi\), and the empty graph \(\mathbf 0\).

\[ \begin{aligned} & f_{\mathbf R_s} & &\text{is loyal to} & \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}\\ & f_{\mathbf T_s} & &\text{is loyal to} & \mathbf{0}\\ & f_{\mathbf M_s} & &\text{is loyal to} & \mathbf{\Psi}_{s}\\ & f_{\mathbf V_s} & &\text{is loyal to} & \mathbf{0}\\ \end{aligned} \]

Homogeneity 1: Homogeneous random network interference

We assume that the exposure mapping for each variable in the structural part of the model is separable and component functions are network exposure mappings with respect to \(\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Structural } (\mathbb{R}^n) \\ & \mathbf R_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf R_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf R_{s}}, \mathbf g_{VR}(\mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) ) \\ & \mathbf T_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf T_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf T_{s}}, \mathbf R_{s}) \\ & \mathbf M_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf M_s} (\mathbf U_{\mathbf M_{s}} , \mathbf V_{s-1}, \mathbf M_{s-1}, \mathbf g_{TM}(\mathbf{T}_{s}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s})) \\ & \mathbf V_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf V_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf V_{s}}, \mathbf M_{s}, \mathbf V_{s-1}) \\ \text{Relational } (\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}) \\ & \mathbf{\Psi}_{s} & &= F_{\mathbf{\Psi_s}}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}}, \mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) \\ \end{aligned} \tag{C.11}\]

where

\[ \mathbf g_{*}(\mathbf{V}^*, \mathbf{\Psi}^*) = \begin{bmatrix} g_{*}(\mathbf{V}^*, \Psi^*_1)\\ g_{*}(\mathbf{V}^*, \Psi^*_2) \\ ... \\ g_{*}(\mathbf{V}^*, \Psi^*_n) \\ \end{bmatrix} \] and \(\Psi^*_k\) is the \(k^{th}\) row of the adjacency matrix \(\mathbf \Psi^*\). The functions \(g_{*}\) are network exposure mappings.

Homogeneity 2: Identical distributions

We assume that all structural equations contained in the vector \(\mathbf f_{*}\) are identical.

\[ \mathbf f_{*}(\mathbf{U}_*, \mathbf{Pa(*)}) = \begin{bmatrix} f_{*}(U_{*_1}, \mathbf{Pa}(*_1))\\ f_{*}(U_{*_2}, \mathbf{Pa}(*_2))\\ ... \\ f_{*}(U_{*_n}, \mathbf{Pa}(*_n))\\ \end{bmatrix} \]

Homogeneity 3: Bipartite network

We assume that \(\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}\) is bipartite. This means that we can represent it as a \(p \times q\) matrix. We can also represent place attributes using vectors of length \(q\), and person attributes using vectors of length \(p\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Structural: Places } (\mathbb{R}^q) \\ & \mathbf R_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf R_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf R_{s}}, \mathbf g_{VR}(\mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) ) \\ & \mathbf T_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf T_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf T_{s}}, \mathbf R_{s}) \\ \text{Structural: People } (\mathbb{R}^p) \\ & \mathbf M_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf M_s} (\mathbf U_{\mathbf M_{s}} , \mathbf V_{s-1}, \mathbf M_{s-1}, \mathbf g_{TM}(\mathbf{T}_{s}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s})) \\ & \mathbf V_{s} & &= f_{\mathbf V_s}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf V_{s}}, \mathbf M_{s}, \mathbf V_{s-1}) \\ \text{Relational } (\mathbb{R}^{p \times q}) \\ & \mathbf{\Psi}_{s} & &= F_{\mathbf{\Psi_s}}(\mathbf U_{\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}}, \mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) \\ \end{aligned} \tag{C.12}\]

with \(g_{VR}\) and \(g_{TM}\) redefined to be conformable.

Homogeneity 4: Identical and independent attachment

We assume each row of the adjacency matrix \({\Psi}_{si}\) is generated by the same \(q \times 1\) array of structural equations \(F_{{\mathbf \Psi_s}}\). We further assume that \(\mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{si}}\), the rows of the \(p \times q\) matrix of error terms \(\mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{s}}\), are independent. This implies that each person in the model attaches to a selection of places independently of any other person and based on identical structural equations.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Structural: Places } (\mathbb{R}) \\ & R_{sj} & &= f_{ R_s}( U_{ R_{sj}}, g_{VR}(\mathbf{V}_{s-1}, {[\mathbf \Psi^T_s]}_{j}) ) \\ & T_{sj} & &= f_{ T_s}( U_{ T_{sj}}, R_{sj}) \\ \text{Structural: People } (\mathbb{R}) \\ & M_{si} & &= f_{ M_s} ( U_{ M_{si}} , V_{s-1,i}, M_{s-1, i}, g_{TM}(\mathbf{T}_{s}, {\Psi}_{si})) \\ & V_{si} & &= f_{ V_s}( U_{ V_{si}}, M_{si}, V_{s-1, i}) \\ \text{Relational: People in places } (\mathbb{R}^{1 \times q}) \\ & {\Psi}_{si} & &= F_{{\mathbf \Psi_s}}(\mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{si}}, \mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) \\ \end{aligned} \]

where \({[\mathbf \Psi^T_s]}_{j}\) is the \(j^{th}\) row of the transpose of the adjacency matrix \(\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}\) and

\[ \mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{si}} \perp \mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{sj}} \text{ for } i \neq j \]

Homogeneity 5: Time homogeneity

Finally, we assume that structural equations are identical across time points \(s\), with the exception of the random variables defined for \(s = 0\). Each of these has no parents.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Structural: Places } (\mathbb{R}) \\ & R_{0j} & &= f_{ R_0}( U_{ R_{0j}}) \\ & T_{0j} & &= f_{ T_0}( U_{ T_{0j}}) \\ & R_{sj} & &= f_{ R}( U_{ R_{sj}}, g_{VR}(\mathbf{V}_{s-1}, {[\mathbf \Psi^T_s]}_{j}) ) \\ & T_{sj} & &= f_{ T}( U_{ T_{sj}}, R_{sj}) \\ \text{Structural: People } (\mathbb{R}) \\ & M_{0i} & &= f_{ M_0} ( U_{ M_{0i}}) \\ & V_{0i} & &= f_{ V_0}( U_{ V_{0i}}) \\ & M_{si} & &= f_{ M} ( U_{ M_{si}} , V_{s-1,i}, M_{s-1, i}, g_{TM}(\mathbf{T}_{s}, {\Psi}_{si})) \\ & V_{si} & &= f_{ V}( U_{ V_{si}}, M_{si}, V_{s-1, i}) \\ \text{Relational: People in places } (\mathbb{R^{1 \times q}}) \\ & {\Psi}_{0i} & &= F_{{\mathbf \Psi_0}}(\mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{0i}}) \\ & {\Psi}_{si} & &= F_{{\mathbf \Psi}}(\mathbf U_{{\Psi}_{si}}, \mathbf{V}_{s-1}, \mathbf{\Psi}_{s-1}) \\ \end{aligned} \]

C.3.3 Remarks

This section introduced the Random Bipartite Network FFRCISTG, extending the framework to settings where both the network structure and the relationships between units are treated as random variables. By doing so, we transformed the fixed-network setting of homogeneous interference into one that approximates independent and identically distributed (IID) sampling.

In this formulation, each observation corresponds to an individual node with its own set of measures and network exposure values. Because the underlying structure is bipartite, linking individuals and places, and because all places are fully ascertained, it is possible to determine the complete set of exposure values for every included person (as opposed to place) observation.

This structure makes it possible to imagine a super-population in which individuals are independently censored from the observed sample but would each have fully observed exposure mappings. In this sense, the random bipartite model recovers many of the inferential properties of IID sampling while preserving the dependencies that define network-based causal processes.

In the next section, we show how a model of this form can be used to coordinate a real outbreak response—whether through targeted vaccination, screening referral, or intensified information dissemination—demonstrating how network-based causal models can inform efficient and equitable public health intervention.

C.4 Vaccine strategy model

We used data from RESPND-MI to assess the effectiveness of two approaches to conducting mass vaccination campaigns quickly. MPX NYC is an online survey of about 1300 individuals that was conducted in 2022 in New York City. Study participants were asked to indicate their home on a map and were asked to indicate places they had had social contact in a congregate setting. Spatial coordinates were converted into community district identifiers. Community districts spatially partition the city of New York.

In the SWING shown in Figure C.13 and Equation C.14, we show an intervention which replaces the function for the risk rating for each community (\(\mathbf{R}\)) with the function \(h\). Every variable that is downstream of the first variable created using \(h\) is marked with a superscript \((h)\) to show that it is partially determined by that function.

We ask what the impact of taking policy \(h\) would be on the decision whether or not to vaccinate a neighborhood \(j\) at time \(s\) (\(T_{js}\)). We can also ask what the impact of the policy is on the exposure of participant \(i\) to the campaign message at time \(s\) (\(M_{is}\)).

Our interest in RESPND-MI, however, was initially to provide evidence to inform a targeted vaccination campaign, so the main outcomes of interest are the vaccination status of each individual \(i\) at time \(s\) (\(V^{(h)}_{is}\)). We were interested in contrasting two approaches to assigning risk-ratings to community districts: the contact-neutralizing approach and the movement-neutralizing approach.

Whereas the contact-neutralizing approach ranks community districts by how many individuals are connected to them, the movement-neutralizing approach ranks community districts by how centrally they are connected to other community districts through the exchange of individuals.

C.4.1 Assumptions

We make the simplifying assumption that only one community district is vaccinated at a time - the top-ranked one. We make the further simplifying assumptions that every person associated with a community district (either through residence or social activity) receives the campaign message when a community district is vaccinated, and that every person who receives a campaign message gets vaccinated. Once an individual is vaccinated, they remain vaccinated.

C.4.2 Structural equations

According to the model specified by structural equations C.14, no individual \(i\) has been vaccinated at time 0, and nobody receives the campaign message. The risk rating for community district \(j\) at time \(s\) is some function \(h\) of the vaccination status at time \(s-1\) for each individual who is connected to that community district. An intervention is conducted in community district \(j\) as time \(s\) if that community district has the highest risk rating. Each individual \(i\) is said to have received the campaign message at time \(s\) if the intervention was conducted in any of the community districts he is connected to. Finally, we consider individual \(i\) vaccinated at time \(s\) if they were vaccinated at time \(s-1\) or if they received the campaign message in time \(s\). The risk rating is given by the function \(h\).

C.4.3 Estimands

Spatial efficiency

Intervention coverage for the \(s^{th}\) community district to be immunized, is defined:

\[ C_s^{(h)} = \frac{1}{|\mathscr{N}_p|}\sum_i V_{si}^{(h)} \]

The cumulative intervention coverage after immunizing \(s\) community districts is therefore:

\[ \bar{C_s}^{(h)} = \sum_{v = 1}^s{C_v }^{(h)} \]

Social network impact

Define graph \(G^{s}(N, \psi_s)\) such that:

\[ \psi_s(i, j) = \psi(i, j) \times (1 -V_{(s-1), i}) \]

The cumulative social network impact of immunizing \(s\) community districts is measured in terms of the size of the largest of the connected components \(LCC_{(s)}\) of the \(G^{s}(N, \psi_s)\) network as a proportion of the total number of participants.

\[ \frac{LCC_s}{|N_p|} \]

C.4.4 Estimation

We simulated the intervention outlined above using RESPND-MI data by calculating the \(G^{s}(N, \psi_s)\) where \(s\) ranges from \(1\) to the total number of community districts. We measured the uncertainty of our estimates by re-sampling study participants with replacement.
